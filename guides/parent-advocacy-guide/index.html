Section 1

Know Your Rights as a New York Parent

As the parent of a child who may need special education services in New York, federal and state law give you powerful, enforceable rights. Understanding them is the first step to effective advocacy.

The Legal Framework Two laws protect your child: the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act (IDEA) (federal) and New York Education Law Article 89 (state). Together they guarantee a Free Appropriate Public Education (FAPE) in the Least Restrictive Environment (LRE).
Your Right What It Means Status
Prior Written Notice The district must notify you in writing before making any change to your child's identification, evaluation, or placement. Required
Procedural Safeguards You must receive a copy of the Procedural Safeguards Notice at least once per year, and at other key moments. Required
Informed Consent The district cannot evaluate your child for the first time or initially place them in special education without your written consent. Required
Independent Educational Evaluation (IEE) If you disagree with the district's evaluation, you can request an IEE at public expense. The district must either fund it or initiate a hearing to defend their evaluation. Your Right
Participate in Meetings You are a full IEP team member. Meetings must be scheduled at a mutually agreed time and place. Your Right
Access Records You have the right to inspect and receive copies of all educational records within 45 days of request. Your Right
Advocacy Tip: Request a copy of the Procedural Safeguards Notice from your district and read it carefully. In NYC, the DOE's safeguards document is available in over 12 languages at schools.nyc.gov.
Section 2

First Steps: What to Do When You Have Concerns

If you suspect your child may need special education services, taking deliberate, documented steps from the very beginning will make a significant difference. Do not wait for the school to raise concerns — under NY law, parents have the right to initiate the process.

1

Document Your Concerns in Writing

Write down specific examples of what you're observing at home — academic struggles, behavioral challenges, developmental differences. Date your notes. This becomes your baseline record.

2

Talk to the Classroom Teacher

Request a meeting with your child's teacher to understand what they're observing in school. Ask specifically: "Is my child receiving any supports? Has a referral been considered?" Follow up every verbal conversation with a brief email summary.

3

Request Intervention Documentation

Ask the school what tiered interventions (RTI/MTSS) are currently in place. You are entitled to data showing your child's response to general education interventions before a special education referral is made.

4

Submit a Formal Evaluation Request

If concerns persist, submit a written request for a special education evaluation directly to the school principal and the CSE Chairperson. This starts the official 60-day evaluation clock under New York law. See our full Evaluation Request Guide.

5

Keep a Dedicated File

Create a physical or digital binder for all school-related documents: report cards, test scores, teacher notes, emails, IEPs, and evaluation reports. Organization is one of the most powerful advocacy tools you have.

Do Not Rely on Verbal Agreements: Any agreement, commitment, or promise made at a meeting should be confirmed in writing — either via email the same day or in the IEP document itself. Verbal assurances are not enforceable.
Section 3

The Evaluation Process

Once you submit a written evaluation request (or the school refers your child), New York law sets strict timelines that the district must follow. Understanding these timelines is critical — delays are a common rights violation.

Day 0

Parent Submits Written Evaluation Request

Send via certified mail or email to both the school principal and the CSE Chairperson. Keep a copy of everything you send.

Within 10 School Days

District Sends Consent Form

The district must provide you with a "Permission to Evaluate" form. Review it carefully — it specifies which assessments will be conducted.

You Sign Consent

60-School-Day Clock Starts

The moment you return signed consent, the official 60-school-day evaluation window begins. The district must complete all evaluations and hold an initial CSE meeting within this period.

Before the CSE Meeting

Receive Evaluation Reports

You are entitled to receive copies of all evaluation reports at least 5 days prior to the CSE meeting. Do not attend a CSE meeting without having reviewed these reports in advance — you can request a postponement.

Within 60 School Days

Initial CSE Meeting Held

The IEP team meets, reviews evaluation results, determines eligibility, and (if eligible) develops the Initial IEP. Services must begin within 60 school days of receiving consent.

Track the Clock: Note the exact date you submitted your evaluation request. If the district misses the 60-school-day deadline, this is a procedural violation of IDEA that may entitle your child to compensatory services. Contact an advocate immediately.

You have the right to request that additional evaluations be conducted beyond what the district proposes — for example, a speech-language evaluation, occupational therapy evaluation, or assistive technology evaluation — if you believe they are relevant to your child's needs.

Section 4

Mastering CSE Meetings

The Committee on Special Education (CSE) meeting is where critical decisions about your child's education are made. Many parents feel overwhelmed or outnumbered — the room is often filled with school staff. Preparation is your greatest equalizer.

Before the Meeting

  • Request and review all evaluation reports at least 5 days in advance. Take detailed notes on areas you have questions about.
  • Write down your top 3–5 priorities for your child's program before you walk in the door.
  • You may bring a support person — a trusted friend, family member, or private advocate. Notify the school in advance.
  • Request an agenda. You can also submit agenda items you want discussed in writing ahead of the meeting.
  • Review the previous IEP (for annual reviews) and note which goals were met, partially met, or not addressed.

During the Meeting

  • You have the right to audio record the meeting with advance notice to the school (in NYC, notify the school at least 24 hours before).
  • Slow down and ask for clarification on any term or acronym you don't understand. Do not feel pressured to sign anything at the meeting.
  • If the team proposes something you disagree with, calmly say: "I'm not in agreement with that recommendation. I'd like to understand the data supporting it before I respond."
  • Take notes or have your support person take notes throughout the meeting.
  • Ask at the end: "What are the next steps, and what are the timelines?"
Your Consent Rights: You are not required to sign the IEP at the meeting. Under IDEA, you have the right to take the draft IEP home, review it, consult with an advocate or attorney, and provide written consent separately. Do not let anyone pressure you to sign on the spot.

After the Meeting

  • Send a follow-up email within 48 hours summarizing key points, any agreements reached, and outstanding questions.
  • You must receive a copy of the final IEP within 60 days. If you do not, follow up in writing.
  • Review the final IEP document carefully to ensure it matches what was discussed. Discrepancies must be addressed immediately in writing.
Section 5

IEP Essentials: What to Look For

An Individualized Education Program (IEP) is a legally binding document. Every element should be specifically tailored to your child — if it reads like it could apply to any student, push back. Vague IEPs produce vague services.

Present Levels of Performance (PLOP)

The PLOP should describe your child's current academic achievement and functional performance with specific, measurable data — not general statements. Look for actual grade-level data, percentile scores, and qualitative observations.

Annual Goals

Each goal must be measurable. A strong goal includes: a baseline, a target behavior, a level of accuracy (e.g., 80%), and a timeframe. If a goal says "the student will improve reading," ask how improvement will be measured and verified.

Special Education Services

Services must specify the type, frequency (e.g., 3x/week), duration (e.g., 45 minutes), location (push-in vs. pull-out), and start date. Vague service descriptions are a red flag — "as needed" is not a legal service specification.

Accommodations & Modifications

Accommodations (extended time, preferential seating) change how your child accesses learning. Modifications change what they are expected to learn. Both should be listed specifically, not in a general list. Confirm they are documented for state testing.

Placement Recommendation & LRE

Placement must be in the Least Restrictive Environment (LRE) where your child can receive a FAPE. If a more restrictive setting is recommended, the IEP must explain why a less restrictive environment is not appropriate. Push for this justification to be specific and data-driven.

Section 6

Dispute Resolution Options

When you and the school district cannot agree, New York provides several formal and informal dispute resolution options. Understanding each option — and its strategic implications — is essential before you choose a path.

A

Informal Resolution: Direct Communication

Always attempt to resolve disagreements first by communicating directly with the CSE Chairperson or building administrator in writing. Many disputes are resolved at this stage, and it establishes a paper trail if escalation becomes necessary.

B

State Complaint (NYSED)

You can file a written complaint with the New York State Education Department (NYSED) alleging that the district violated a specific provision of IDEA or Article 89. NYSED must investigate and issue a written decision within 60 calendar days. No attorney is required, and there is no cost. This is most effective for systemic or procedural violations.

C

Mediation

A voluntary, free process facilitated by a neutral third party. Both the parent and district must agree to participate. Mediation agreements are legally binding. This is the fastest formal resolution method — often resolved within 30 days. Mediation does not waive your right to a hearing.

D

Impartial Hearing (Due Process)

The most formal option — a hearing before an Impartial Hearing Officer (IHO). You must file a Due Process Complaint Notice. A resolution meeting is required within 15 days. Hearings are conducted like legal proceedings — most parents are represented by an attorney. If you prevail, you may be entitled to attorney's fees. Explore our full Dispute Resolution Guide for details on the NYC Impartial Hearing process.

Statute of Limitations: In New York, you generally have 2 years from the date you knew or should have known about the violation to file a Due Process complaint. Do not delay — consult an advocate or attorney promptly if you believe your child's rights have been violated.
Section 7

Sample Advocacy Letters

Written communication is the foundation of special education advocacy. Below are two templates to help you get started. Always send important correspondence via email (creating a timestamp) or certified mail.

Template A: Request for Special Education Evaluation

Copy & Customize
[Your Name]
[Your Address]
[Date]

[CSE Chairperson Name]
Committee on Special Education
[School District Name]

Dear [CSE Chairperson Name],

I am the parent/guardian of [Child's Full Name], DOB [Date of Birth], currently enrolled in grade [Grade] at [School Name].

I am writing to formally request a comprehensive special education evaluation for my child pursuant to the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act (IDEA), 20 U.S.C. § 1414, and New York Education Law Article 89. I have concerns regarding my child's [describe specific area: reading, attention, speech, social-emotional development, etc.], which are negatively affecting their educational performance.

I understand that once I provide written consent, the district has 60 school days to complete all evaluations and hold an initial CSE meeting. I respectfully request that evaluations include [list specific evaluations you are requesting, e.g., psychoeducational, speech-language, OT].

Please contact me at [phone/email] to provide the Permission to Evaluate form and schedule next steps.

Sincerely,
[Your Signature]
[Your Printed Name]

Template B: Post-Meeting Follow-Up Email

Send Within 48 Hours of Any CSE Meeting

To: [CSE Chairperson Email]
Cc: [School Principal Email]
Subject: Follow-Up: CSE Meeting for [Child's Name], held [Date]

Dear [CSE Chairperson Name],

Thank you for meeting with me on [Date] regarding [Child's Name]. I am writing to confirm my understanding of the meeting's outcomes:

1. [First key point or agreement reached]
2. [Second key point — e.g., "The team agreed to add 30 minutes/week of speech therapy"]
3. [Outstanding items — e.g., "I requested additional OT evaluation; awaiting written response"]

I also want to note that I did not consent to [any item you did not agree to] at this time and will provide written consent separately.

Please correct me if I have mischaracterized any aspect of our discussion. I look forward to receiving the draft IEP for review within the required timeframe.

Sincerely,
[Your Name]
[Phone Number]
