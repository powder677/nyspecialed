Navigating Special Education in Hempstead UFSD: A Parent's Guide

Understanding the special education process in Hempstead Union Free School District (UFSD) can be challenging. This guide provides information and resources to help parents advocate effectively for their children with disabilities. Remember, you are your child's best advocate, and knowing your rights under the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act (IDEA) and New York State Education Law is crucial.

Key Steps in the Special Education Process in Hempstead UFSD

  1. Referral: The process begins with a referral for special education services. This can be initiated by parents, teachers, doctors, or other professionals. NYSED's Parent Guide to Special Education provides further information on referrals.
  2. Evaluation: Upon receiving a referral, the district must conduct a comprehensive individual evaluation to determine if your child has a disability and what their educational needs are. This evaluation must assess all areas related to the suspected disability (34 CFR §300.304). Parents have the right to obtain an Independent Educational Evaluation (IEE) at public expense if they disagree with the district's evaluation, under certain conditions (8 NYCRR §200.5(g)). Advocacy Tip: Don't hesitate to request an IEE if you feel the school's evaluation is incomplete or inaccurate. Document your reasons for disagreeing with the school's evaluation in writing.
  3. Eligibility Determination: The Committee on Special Education (CSE) reviews the evaluation results and determines if your child is eligible for special education services. You, as parents, are crucial members of the CSE (8 NYCRR §200.3(a)(3)). Make sure your voice is heard!
  4. IEP Development: If your child is found eligible, the CSE develops an Individualized Education Program (IEP). The IEP outlines your child's present levels of performance, goals, services, and accommodations. This is a collaborative process, and you have the right to participate fully in its development (34 CFR §300.322). Advocacy Tip: Come prepared to the IEP meeting with your own observations, data, and proposed goals. Don't be afraid to challenge the district's recommendations if you believe they are not in your child's best interest. Remember, the IEP should be tailored to meet *your child's unique needs*.
  5. IEP Implementation: The school district is responsible for implementing the IEP as written. Regularly monitor your child's progress and communicate with teachers and related service providers.
  6. Annual Review: The IEP is reviewed at least annually to assess progress and make necessary adjustments.
  7. Re-evaluation: Your child must be re-evaluated at least every three years (triennial), or more frequently if needed, to determine continued eligibility and to update the IEP (34 CFR §300.303).

Common Pitfalls and Advocacy Strategies in Hempstead UFSD

  • Inadequate Evaluations: Ensure that the district's evaluations are comprehensive and address all areas of concern. Push back if you feel testing is rushed or insufficient. Specifically request evaluations aligned with your child's needs (e.g., speech-language, occupational therapy, psychological, neuropsychological).
  • Predetermined IEPs: Be wary of IEPs that seem pre-written before the meeting. The IEP should be a result of collaborative discussion and tailored to your child. NYSED prohibits predetermined IEPs.
  • Lack of Appropriate Services: Advocate for the specific services your child needs, even if the district claims they are unavailable. Districts are required to provide a Free Appropriate Public Education (FAPE) which means providing specialized instruction and related services to meet the unique needs of a child with a disability (34 CFR §300.17). This may involve services such as speech therapy, occupational therapy, counseling, assistive technology, and specialized academic instruction.
  • Placement Issues: Ensure your child is placed in the Least Restrictive Environment (LRE). This means they should be educated with their non-disabled peers to the maximum extent appropriate (34 CFR §300.114). Question placements that seem overly restrictive without a clear educational justification.
  • Discipline Issues: Understand your child's rights regarding discipline. Students with disabilities have specific protections under IDEA, particularly regarding suspensions exceeding 10 days. A manifestation determination review is required if the suspension is related to the child's disability (34 CFR §300.530).
  • Transition Planning: For students aged 16 and older (or younger, if appropriate), the IEP must include transition planning to prepare them for life after high school. This includes goals related to post-secondary education, employment, and independent living (34 CFR §300.321).

Step-by-Step Guide: Requesting an Independent Educational Evaluation (IEE)

  1. Review the District's Evaluation: Carefully examine the district's evaluation report and identify the areas with which you disagree.
  2. Submit a Written Request: Send a formal written request to the CSE chairperson for an IEE at public expense. Clearly state your reasons for disagreeing with the district's evaluation. Refer to 8 NYCRR §200.5(g) to support your request.
  3. District Response: The district has the right to either grant your request or initiate an impartial hearing to demonstrate that their evaluation was appropriate.
  4. IEE Selection: If the district grants your request, they must provide you with a list of qualified evaluators. You can also propose your own evaluator, but the district must agree to them. The evaluator must meet specific qualifications, including being licensed or certified.
  5. Evaluation Process: Coordinate with the evaluator to schedule and conduct the IEE. Ensure the evaluator is aware of your child's IEP and relevant background information.
  6. IEE Report: Obtain a written report from the evaluator.
  7. Present the IEE to the CSE: Share the IEE report with the CSE and request that it be considered in the development or revision of your child's IEP. The district must consider the results of the IEE (8 NYCRR §200.5(g)).

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

Q: What do I do if I disagree with the IEP developed by the CSE?
A: You have several options. First, clearly articulate your concerns at the IEP meeting and request revisions. Document your disagreements in writing. If you are still not satisfied, you can request mediation or file a complaint with the New York State Education Department (NYSED). You also have the right to request an impartial hearing to challenge the IEP (34 CFR §300.511).
Q: How can I prepare for a CSE meeting?
A: Review your child's records, including evaluations, IEPs, and progress reports. Write down your concerns, goals, and proposed services. Gather any relevant data, such as observations or work samples. Consider bringing a friend, advocate, or attorney to the meeting for support. Prepare questions in advance.
Q: What is the difference between an IEP and a 504 plan?
A: An IEP is for students with disabilities who require specialized instruction. A 504 plan is for students with disabilities who do not require specialized instruction but need accommodations to access the general education curriculum. IEPs are governed by IDEA, while 504 plans are governed by Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act.
Q: My child is being bullied at school. What are my rights?
A: Schools have a responsibility to address bullying. If your child is being bullied because of their disability, it may be considered disability-based harassment. Report the bullying to the school principal and the CSE. Request that the IEP be modified to address the bullying and provide support for your child. NYSED provides resources on bullying prevention and intervention.
Q: The school district is not following my child's IEP. What can I do?
A: Document the instances where the IEP is not being followed. Communicate your concerns to the school principal and the CSE chairperson in writing. Request a meeting to discuss the issue and develop a plan to ensure IEP implementation. If the problem persists, you can file a complaint with NYSED or pursue legal action.
